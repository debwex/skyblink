See how the sky changes — by sight or by sound.

Type any spot in the sky and SKY BLINK plays NASA SPHEREx's images of it like a little video, so moving and changing objects jump out: comets, asteroids, nearby stars, brown dwarfs, maybe even a hidden Planet X. Every view can also be heard, so blind and low-vision explorers can join the hunt.

    What changed?

      Change finder compares the first and last frames. A red/blue pair close together usually means something moved; a single spot means something got brighter or dimmer. Spots with no Gaia star nearby are the most interesting. Careful: each frame is a slightly different infrared colour, so some stars change brightness just because of that. Check a candidate in Blink before you believe it!

      Spectrum on the fly

      Each dot is one SPHEREx image, measured at the exact colour where the target landed. Pink dashed lines mark colours where water (1.4 µm) and methane (3.3 µm) absorb light; grey lines mark carbon dioxide (4.26 µm) and carbon monoxide (4.67 µm). A cold brown dwarf like Epsilon Indi B looks dark at those colours and bright in between: that is how SPHEREx reveals methane.

      Step 2 · Explore · Planet X Hunter and the challenge targets

      Pick a target and blink between SPHEREx visits

      SPHEREx maps the whole sky every six months. Compare its images to catch Solar System worlds and nearby stars and brown dwarfs moving across the sky, and stars that brighten and dim. Start with Planet X Hunter: this is how a hidden planet would give itself away.

      🪐 What is Planet X Hunter?

      1. The mystery. Some astronomers think an undiscovered planet, nicknamed Planet X (or "Planet Nine"), may orbit far beyond Neptune. Nobody has found it yet.
      2. How it would be found. Stars are so far away that they stay still. Anything in our Solar System moves between images. In 1930, Clyde Tombaugh was searching for "Planet X" this way when he found Pluto. Pluto was later reclassified as a dwarf planet (2006), and the search for a bigger hidden planet goes on.
      3. Our test. Pluto is the perfect practice target: a real, far, slow-moving world, just like Planet X would be. We use real NASA SPHEREx images of it from October 2025, with positions from NASA JPL Horizons. Press ▶ Blink: the stars stay still and Pluto walks across the picture. Press 🎧 Listen to hear it move.
      4. How far could SPHEREx see it? Use the distance slider below the picture: even at 500 times Earth's distance from the Sun, Planet X would still drift ~8 SPHEREx pixels in a week.

      Planet X Hunter: how far away could SPHEREx catch it moving?

      Planets far from the Sun seem to drift because Earth moves around the Sun, like nearby trees sliding past a train window faster than far-away hills. The farther the world, the slower it drifts.

      Estimate for a world seen roughly at right angles to Earth's motion; real motion varies through the year. Planet Nine, a proposed Planet X, is predicted at about 400–800 AU.

      Behind the scenes

      How SKY BLINK works

      Our own Python code turns raw NASA archive files into pictures and sounds anyone can explore. The steps below show the 3I/ATLAS case study; Search and Planet X Hunter use the same steps on SPHEREx Data Release 1 images (live search runs through our Python helper).

      Honest limits

      • "Comet signal" is signal-to-noise inside a small circle — great for comparing colours, but not a calibrated brightness.
      • Each image only sees one infrared colour at the comet's spot, so the fingerprint is built from many images taken at different times.
      • Known-asteroid positions come from NASA JPL predictions for an observer on Earth; SPHEREx orbits Earth, so labels can be off by a pixel or two.
      • The change finder only flags candidates. Different frames see slightly different infrared colours, so always check a candidate by blinking before believing it.
      • SPHEREx pixels are large (6.15″). Very faint targets (like WISE 0855) are not detected, and very bright stars (like R Draconis) saturate the images.
      • On this website, Search shows saved real searches of the 5 challenge targets. Searching any other position needs our Python helper running on a computer.

      About

      About SKY BLINK

      What we built

      SKY BLINK turns real NASA SPHEREx data into a tool anyone can use — by sight or by sound. Search fetches SPHEREx images of any sky position from NASA/IPAC IRSA, lines them up, blinks them and finds changes. Planet X Hunter shows Pluto and Makemake moving in real SPHEREx images, and the 3I/ATLAS case study measures an interstellar comet at the exact infrared colour of each image. Every view can also be heard.

      Team

      Kepler 16 — a student team from Miri, Malaysia. NASA Space Apps Challenge 2026.

      Data and credits

      • SPHEREx Data Release 1 (spherex_dr1) and Quick Release (QR2) spectral images, NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA).
      • Pluto and Makemake positions: NASA JPL Horizons.
      • IRSA table of SPHEREx observations of comet 3I/ATLAS.
      • Known asteroids: NASA JPL Small-Body Identification API.
      • Star positions and motions: ESA Gaia Data Release 3 (SIMBAD for brown dwarfs too faint for Gaia). Infrared sources: AllWISE (NASA/IPAC IRSA).
      • Name lookup: CDS Sesame; sky atlas links: CDS Aladin Lite.
      • Inspired by WiseView, the public blink tool for NASA's WISE mission.
      • Python: astropy, astroquery, reproject, numpy, scipy, matplotlib, Pillow. Sound: Web Audio API and the browser speech voice.

      This project makes use of data products from SPHEREx, which is a joint project of the Jet Propulsion Laboratory and the California Institute of Technology, and is funded by the National Aeronautics and Space Administration.
